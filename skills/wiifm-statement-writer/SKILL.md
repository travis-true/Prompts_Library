# WIIFM Statement Writer

Write WIIFM statements that a busy, skeptical employee would actually believe in the first 30-60 seconds of a session. The output is learner-centered, specific, honest, and short enough to say aloud.

## What a WIIFM is (and is not)

A WIIFM answers one question from the learner's seat: **"Why should I spend my time on this?"**

It is **not**:
- A list of learning objectives ("You will be able to define, list, describe...").
- A statement of what the organization wants ("To ensure compliance and align with strategic goals...").
- A course description ("This session covers...").

Think of it as short, honest advertising for the learner's time. Objectives say what learners will do; the WIIFM says why that matters to *them*.

## Required inputs

Two things are required before writing:

1. **Session topic + learning objectives**: what the learner will be able to do afterward.
2. **Audience role + pain points**: who they are, what their day looks like, and what frustrates or worries them that this topic touches.

Helpful but optional: delivery format, duration, how motivated the audience is. Do not ask for these unless they change the writing. Infer a sensible default tone (plain, warm, professional) and move on.

## Step 1: Check the inputs

Judge honestly: could the statement be specific to this audience, or would it end up generic?

- **If both required inputs are specific enough, go straight to Step 2.**
- **If either is thin or vague** (e.g., "managers", "improve communication", no real pain point), ask **1-2 clarifying questions, one at a time**, then write. Do not ask more than two.
  - Prefer the question that most improves specificity, usually the audience's real frustration or the concrete task they'll do differently.
  - Use a clickable/multiple-choice interface if the platform offers one, with 2-4 realistic options. Otherwise use short bulleted options. Always leave room for the user's own answer.
  - Never ask for something the user already gave. Never ask a question whose answer would not change the statement.
- If the user declines to answer or says "just write it," write the best version and list the one or two assumptions you made in a single line at the end.

## Step 2: Choose the angles

Pick the strongest benefit angle for the audience as the **primary**. Pick two *different* angles for the alternates. Each alternate must change the motivation, not just reword the same idea.

| Angle | The learner's gain |
|---|---|
| **Time / effort saved** | Fewer steps, fewer redo cycles, less hunting for answers |
| **Frustration removed** | A specific recurring annoyance goes away |
| **Confidence / competence** | They stop feeling unsure doing something in front of others |
| **Risk / stress avoided** | Fewer errors, escalations, or "did I do that right?" moments |
| **Quality of work / impact** | Better results they can see and be proud of |
| **Credibility / career** | Visible skill that others notice and rely on |

Match the angle to the real pain point. A frustration the audience named beats a generic career benefit.

## Step 3: Write each statement

**Form:** 2-3 sentences, roughly 30-50 words, written to be spoken aloud. Plain conversational language. Present or future tense.

**Structure (flexible, not a template to repeat verbatim):**
1. **The payoff, in the learner's terms**: what gets easier, faster, safer, or better for them, tied to a concrete task from their job.
2. **Why the topic matters to them now**: the cost of the status quo or the moment they'll feel the difference.
3. **(Optional) The bridge**: one phrase connecting it to what they'll do in the session.

**Rules every statement must satisfy:**

- **Learner-centered "you" language.** Lead with what the learner gains, not what the course covers. Benefit before feature.
- **Concrete, measurable payoff.** Name a specific task and an observable result (time saved, errors avoided, steps removed, fewer escalations).
  - **Never invent statistics.** Use a number only if the user supplied it. Otherwise express the payoff as a concrete observable outcome ("finish the weekly report without re-pulling data three times"), not a made-up figure. If a real number would strengthen it, mention that in the closing note.
- **Role-specific.** It should be obviously about this audience. If it could be pasted onto any course, rewrite it.
- **Honest scale.** Promise what a single session can plausibly deliver. No "10x," "transform," or "master."
- **Organizational goals flipped.** If the user's input is business-framed ("reduce rework," "improve CSAT"), translate it to what the learner personally experiences. Employees rarely care about company metrics; they care about their workday.
- **Mandatory or compliance training:** Do not fake enthusiasm. Find the real personal benefit (less stress, fewer mistakes, protecting themselves, clearer rules, avoiding rework) and state it plainly.

**Avoid:**
- Objective verbs as the hook ("define," "list," "identify," "understand").
- Starting with the course name, the agenda, or "In this session we will..."
- Corporate jargon and hype: leverage, synergy, unlock, game-changing, empower, best-in-class, holistic, robust, deep dive, journey, transformative.
- Vague benefits: "be more effective," "improve your skills," "grow professionally."
- Rhetorical questions as filler, exclamation points, and stacked buzzwords.
- Guilt or fear tactics ("you can't afford to miss this").

## Step 4: Self-check, then output

Silently test every draft against this rubric. Rewrite any that fail before showing anything:

1. Is it written from the learner's perspective, not the organization's?
2. Does it make one specific promise a skeptical employee would believe?
3. Is the payoff tied to a real task and an observable outcome, with no invented numbers?
4. Could it be spoken aloud in under ~20 seconds?
5. Does each alternate use a genuinely different angle?
6. Is it free of jargon, hype, and objective-style verbs?

## Output format

Use exactly this structure:

**Primary: [angle label]**
> [2-3 sentence statement]

**Alternate A: [different angle label]**
> [2-3 sentence statement]

**Alternate B: [different angle label]**
> [2-3 sentence statement]

Follow with **at most one short line** only if needed: an assumption you made, or a note that a real figure (time saved, error rate) from the user would strengthen a statement. Skip it if there is nothing worth flagging. Do not add explanations of the principles unless asked.

## Example

**Input:** Topic: building pivot tables in Excel. Objective: summarize a data export into a weekly report. Audience: claims analysts who spend Monday mornings copying and re-sorting data by hand and dread version errors.

**Primary: Time / effort saved**
> You'll turn Monday's export into a finished weekly summary in a few clicks instead of an hour of copying and re-sorting. By the end of this session, you'll have built one from your own data, so next Monday you just refresh it.

**Alternate A: Risk / stress avoided**
> Every manual copy-paste is a chance for a number to land in the wrong row. Today you'll build a summary that recalculates itself, so you can send your report without wondering whether you missed something.

**Alternate B: Confidence / competence**
> When someone asks you to slice the numbers a new way, you'll be able to do it on the spot instead of saying you'll get back to them. You'll leave able to answer those questions live.

## After delivery

If the user asks for changes (shorter, warmer, more direct, different angle, a stronger link to a specific objective), revise the chosen statement and keep the same rules. If they pick one and want it adapted for another channel (slide line, invite blurb, email), do that on request. Do not offer extra formats unprompted.

If the user asks where the WIIFM should appear, a brief answer is fine: state it at the start of the session, and return to it at key points so learners keep connecting the content to their own benefit. Learners also respond better when they hear it before the session, in the invite or pre-work.
