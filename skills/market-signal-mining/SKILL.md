# Market Signal Mining

## Purpose

Turn evidence of buyer friction and spending into a scoped offer hypothesis and validation plan.

Primary audience: Workplace.

## When to use it

Use when you need to turn evidence of buyer friction and spending into a scoped offer hypothesis and validation plan.

Live market findings require browsing or dated source material. The workflow produces a commercial hypothesis, not a sales guarantee.

## Inputs and requirements

Buyer role, market/context, problem, region and currency, budget type, research window, available sources, delivery capability, and permitted proof.

Live market findings require browsing or dated source material. The workflow produces a commercial hypothesis, not a sales guarantee. These instruction-only packages supply no external tools or executable scripts.

## Workflow guide

1. Lock scope before research: one buyer role in one context, one problem, and one possible budget source. Record mandates and deadlines as verified facts or open questions. Ask for missing scope; do not research an unbounded niche.
2. Harvest friction from public primary records, procurement documents, budget disclosures, postmortems, and attributable complaints. Aim for 15–20 relevant findings when available; stop at repeated patterns across independent sources and report coverage gaps. Record source URL, publisher, date, short exact excerpt or labeled paraphrase, buyer, and relevance. Keep quotations brief.
3. Tag evidence SPEND, MANDATE, POSTMORTEM, or COMPLAINT. Prioritize verified spending and funded mandates, but distinguish an allocated budget or mandate from a completed purchase. Separate facts, inferences, and hypotheses. Deduplicate syndicated reports. If browsing is unavailable, use supplied evidence and label currency/coverage limits; never invent findings.
4. Map incumbents and their common failures. Test four questions: does an adjacent budget exist; is the failure attributable to the gap rather than price or effort; what evidence indicates incumbent responses; and can this provider credibly deliver? Mark each Supported, Contradicted, or Unknown. Absence of a competitor finding is not proof of no competition. Keep unsupported gaps provisional.
5. Forge an offer hypothesis: name, buyer, one-sentence promise using supported buyer language, 3–5 delivered components, exclusions, price range in stated currency, comparable-spend anchors with dates, free/low-friction/core/expansion ladder, and budget line. Label prices unvalidated when anchors are missing. Prefer deliverable-based scope; do not force a price without evidence. State the strongest case it will not sell.
6. Inventory proof: direct or adjacent experience, permitted artifacts, and relevant published research. Distinguish personal results from borrowed evidence. Confirm permission before using employer/client material; anonymization alone does not establish permission. Never invent testimonials or imply research endorses the offer.
7. Draft a validation script for 8–10 buyer conversations asking about recent purchases, decision authority, budget line, refresh date, procurement friction, and willingness to take a concrete next step. Treat the source's 30% budget-identification threshold as a provisional screening heuristic, not statistical validation; report actual counts and sample limits. Do not contact buyers without authorization.
8. Return one document under 1,500 words unless requested otherwise: Scope; ranked Findings; Incumbent table; Gap and four tests; Offer; Proof plan; Validation script; Case against; unresolved evidence gaps. Separate an offer hypothesis from a validated commercial result.

## Customize before running

- `[goal]`: The specific result required; for example, a first draft or revision of an existing deliverable.
- `[sources]`: Paste the relevant evidence, draft, or brief; label missing material rather than inventing it.
- `[context]`: Intended reader or user, situation, and use; for example, a workplace content team.
- `[constraints]`: Required format, length, deadline, platform, available tools, and exclusions; use none where absent.

## Example use and output

Fictional scenario: an independent trainer explores onboarding support for small nonprofit operations teams. Illustrative output: Buyer—operations manager; gap hypothesis—handoff instructions are fragmented; budget evidence—Unknown; offer—an onboarding checklist pilot; price—Unvalidated pending comparable purchase evidence; next test—ask which budget funded the last onboarding improvement. No fictional transaction is presented as research.

## Check before using

Every finding is traceable; budgets and purchases are distinguished; competitor claims have scope limits; prices are evidence-backed or labeled hypotheses; all four gap tests are explicit; proof permissions and validation counts are recorded.
